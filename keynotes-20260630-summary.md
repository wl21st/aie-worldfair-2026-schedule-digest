## 1. Swix — Opening Keynote: Loopcraft

**Role:** Co-founder of AI Engineer, Editor at Leighton Space  
**Time:** 9:11 AM

### Summary

Swix kicked off the AI Engineer Worlds Fair with a philosophical yet practical keynote centered on the concept of **Loopcraft** — the core skill of an AI engineer being the ability to define, navigate, and recurse through loops of abstraction. He traced the evolution of AI engineering from the token → chat → tools → goals → automations → cron jobs → loops, framing this as a stack of nested loops that engineers must learn to traverse: going up a loop for broader productivity/scaling inference, and down a loop for debugging and reliability. He noted that too many engineers get stuck operating at a single level of abstraction, unable to zoom out for strategic thinking or zoom in for troubleshooting. The ability to fluidly move between levels is what separates effective AI engineers from the rest.

He introduced a powerful human analogy for understanding loops: the heartbeat (proof of life) → learning to talk → using tools → working → teamwork → broader human projects. This mirrors how AI systems evolve from simple completions to multi-agent orchestration. He emphasized that agents working in one domain are increasingly generalizing across domains — 2026 being the year AI diffuses beyond coding into healthcare, finance, and GTM. He mentioned that the conference added vertical tracks for the first time to reflect this diffusion. Swix also touched on "tiny teams" — the concept that smaller teams augmented by AI can achieve what previously required large organizations. He encouraged engineers to think not just about their personal loops or team loops, but about the broader human project of collective intelligence — what are we building together as a civilization?

Swix shared that attendance grew from ~2,000 (2023) to ~7,000 (2026), reflecting the exponential interest in AI engineering. He joked about the Gini coefficient of ticket buying going in the wrong direction (people buying late) and appealed to future attendees to register earlier. He framed the "highest loop" as humans coming together to figure out what the next loop should be — the conference itself being a "loop that makes loops." He announced AI Engineer summits coming to New York, Paris, Singapore, Melbourne, and Miami. He closed with a call to action: find your generative process for loops and propagate it down to your teams, workflows, and lives — emphasizing that the meta-skill of generating loops is what will compound over time.

### Key Knowledge Points

- **Loopcraft**: The ability to move up and down abstraction loops — up for productivity/scaling, down for reliability/debugging
- **Human Loop Analogy**: Heartbeat → talk → tools → work → teams → civilization-level projects
- **Generalization**: Agents in one domain are generalizing across domains this year
- **Conference Philosophy**: The summit is a meta-loop where the community designs the next loop

### Follow-Up Items

- Read Swix's essay "Loopcraft" for deeper treatment
- Map your current workflow as a stack of loops — identify which level you spend most time at
- Attend AI Engineer summits in New York, Paris, Singapore, Melbourne, or Miami
- Consider how to propagate loop-thinking to your own team and organization

## 2. Pablo Castro (Microsoft) — Knowledge & Agents

**Role:** CVP & Distinguished Engineer, Microsoft  
**Time:** 9:21 AM

### Summary

Pablo Castro presented a framework for thinking about knowledge in AI systems, splitting it into three categories: **intrinsic**, **extrinsic**, and **learned**. Intrinsic knowledge is what's stored in model parameters from training — the source of the exponential we're experiencing. He illustrated this with the evolution of coding tools: IntelliSense (1996) → ranked ML options → GitHub Copilot → ChatGPT → fully agent-generated software (e.g., OpenClaw creating production code with zero hand-written lines). He argued that while intrinsic knowledge kicked off the exponential, it only takes you so far when building agents that need to participate in organizational workflows.

Extrinsic knowledge is external data that grounds agents — this evolved from simple RAG into sophisticated **context engineering** systems. He highlighted Microsoft's approach with **Microsoft Intelligence (IQ)** as a unified entry point to ambient organizational data (SharePoint, email, calendar, chats, data warehouses) and **Fabric IQ** for structured analytics. He noted two key dimensions of evolution: from isolated datasets to company-wide grounding, and from simple vector search to complex retrieval systems. The demo showed creating a **knowledge base** in Microsoft Foundry that ingests unstructured PDFs, structured Parquet tables, and web data — all exposed as an **MCP server** with no glue code. Critically, the knowledge base itself is a standalone asset that any external harness can consume via MCP, not locked into Microsoft's ecosystem. He also discussed token efficiency — carefully evaluating retrieval systems to deliver the most information-dense answer using the fewest tokens.

The third category — **learned knowledge** — is the result of observing processes and improving via reflection. Satya Nadella wrote about how people and agents can compound their work through learning loops that capture what's unique about an organization. Castro demonstrated the **Agent Optimizer** in Foundry, which uses a JEPA-style hill-climbing loop: establish baseline → generate candidates → evaluate → deploy. The optimizer reflects on actual traces from production use to generate improved instructions, skills, and tool definitions without manual prompt engineering. He showed how optimized instructions contained emergent details that were never hand-written — discovered entirely through the hill-climbing process on real usage traces.

### Key Knowledge Points

- **Three Knowledge Types**: Intrinsic (parametric), Extrinsic (retrieved/grounded), Learned (observed/optimized)
- **Context Engineering**: RAG evolved into sophisticated multi-source grounding systems
- **Microsoft IQ**: Unified ambient data access for agents across an organization
- **Agent Optimizer**: JEPA-style loop that hill-climbs agent configurations from production traces
- **Knowledge Base as MCP Server**: Every knowledge base is automatically an MCP server
- **Token Efficiency**: Careful evaluation ensures highest information density per token

### Follow-Up Items

- Explore Microsoft Foundry's knowledge base + MCP server capabilities
- Set up an agent optimizer loop for any production agent
- Evaluate your organization's approach to extrinsic knowledge — are you using a unified entry point?
- Read Satya Nadella's writing on human-agent compounding learning loops
- Try ai.azure.com for hands-on experimentation

## 3. Alexander Embirikos & Roman Hewitt (OpenAI) — Value Maxing

**Role:** Head of Enterprise Product & Head of Developer Experience, OpenAI  
**Time:** 9:40 AM

### Summary

Alexander and Roman delivered an energetic keynote reframing the narrative around AI and engineering. Their core message: **AI engineers are eating the world**, not replacing engineers. Engineering was never about writing code — it's about problem-solving using science, design, taste, judgment, and imagination. The accelerating release cadence (new models roughly every six weeks) and the preview of the **GPT-5.6 series** were key announcements. They noted that last week they launched the 5.6 series preview and are excited to get it into developers' hands.

They introduced the concept of **Value Maxing** — moving beyond token-maxing to focus on extracting maximum business value. Four vectors: **cost efficiency** (GPT-5.6 Tera delivers 5.5-level intelligence at half the cost; Luna at $1/M input tokens and $6/M output tokens — insane value), **speed** (GPT-5.6 Sol on Cerebras at frontier intelligence with dramatically faster token rates), **reliability** (improving with each generation reducing the tension gap), and **quality** (continuous frontier advancement across every eval). They walked through each vector with hard data — Terminal Bench scores, price comparisons, and token throughput numbers.

They discussed the evolution from completions to inline prediction to Command K to agents that test their own work to agents tackling long, hard goals autonomously. Each phase was mind-blowing in its moment but quickly became the new normal. They noted the prediction from Theo that agents will autonomously land PRs within six months — both agreed "much sooner" given the pace of everything. Their key insight: you can't Value Max without being **model-agnostic** and understanding the full cost-per-capability-per-second, not just token pricing. They announced that Claude is now available in Microsoft Foundry, emphasizing interoperability and choice. The session ended with a handoff to Peter Steinberger, whom they introduced as the "Claw Father" who has pushed OpenAI to be "more AGI pilled."

### Key Knowledge Points

- **Engineering != Coding**: Engineering is about problem-solving; AI abstracts coding, amplifying engineers
- **Value Maxing**: Beyond token optimization — measure cost per capability per second
- **GPT-5.6 Series**: Preview announced with Sol (frontier), Tera (cost-efficient), Luna (ultra-low-cost)
- **Release Cadence**: Models shipping every ~6 weeks; frontier labs racing
- **Model Agnosticism**: Critical for value maxing; don't lock into one provider
- **Prediction**: Autonomous PR landing within 6 months (likely sooner)

### Follow-Up Items

- Evaluate your current model mix — are you over-indexing on a single provider?
- Run a cost-per-capability-per-second analysis across your workloads
- Watch for GPT-5.6 general availability and test against your eval suites
- Read Theo's tweet/prediction on autonomous PR landing timelines
- Explore Claude via Microsoft Foundry for interoperability testing

## 4. Peter Steinberger (OpenAI) — From Terminals to Loops

**Role:** "Claw Father," OpenAI (formerly known for pioneering agent loops)  
**Time:** 9:58 AM

### Summary

Peter Steinberger gave one of the most practically insightful talks of the day, tracing his personal journey from terminal-juggling to managing a "small company of agents." He started with a vivid image: himself juggling 10+ terminal windows in January feeling like peak productivity — which today feels "silly." He realized he wasn't orchestrating; he was polling. He was the scheduler, router, and memory.

Three changes made the shift possible: **server-side compaction** (making long-running tasks reliable), **coordination** (one thread creates and steers projects), and **automation/triggers** (waking managers asynchronously). The result: persistent context, delegation, and triggers form the loop. Once the loop works, the bottleneck keeps moving: tokens → compute → **tension** (human attention). Unlike tokens or compute, you can't add more tension — so deciding where to spend it is the most important skill.

He described a realistic workflow: issue filed → manager agent wakes up, reads against project goals → decides if it's a fit → creates worker agent → worker investigates, implements, tests → another agent reviews → returns PR + diff + video + running build. The human reviews once, leaves a note, approves. The agent runs the inner execution loop; the human sets direction in the outer loop. He cited Paul Salt running a version where a "chief of staff" agent wakes every 5 minutes to coordinate GitHub work.

His vision: agents shouldn't be trapped inside a laptop or app. An agent should be textable, reachable from Slack, hearable from anywhere. "The future is not terminals — it's better loops."

### Key Knowledge Points

- **Three Enablers**: Server-side compaction, coordination (one thread steers projects), automation/triggers
- **Bottleneck Migration**: Tokens → Compute → Tension (human attention is the new scarcity)
- **Manager Pattern**: Long-lived manager agent delegates to worker agents; human sets direction in outer loop
- **Inner/Outer Loop**: Agent runs inner execution loop; human makes decisions in the outer loop
- **Cross-Host Mobility**: Agents should move between machines, not be tied to a laptop
- **Agent as Entity**: Should be textable, reachable from Slack, not trapped in a terminal session

### Follow-Up Items

- Implement a "chief of staff" agent pattern for your GitHub workflow
- Set up server-side compaction for long-running agent tasks
- Move from watching agents work to reviewing their output asynchronously
- Explore cross-host agent mobility (Codex gateway, OpenClaw nodes)
- Read more about Paul Salt's pinned chief-of-staff implementation
- Design a manager-worker agent architecture for your team

## 5. Zhuan Li (ZAI/GLM) — GLM 5.2 & Open Weight Philosophy

**Role:** ZAI/GLM (joined remotely from China)  
**Time:** 10:08 AM

### Summary

Zhuan Li presented GLM (General Language Model) from ZAI, clarifying that "GLM" is not a brand name but an architecture — auto-regressive blank filling, published in 2021, making them one of the earliest labs exploring LLMs alongside OpenAI, Anthropic, and DeepMind. He noted that even though they no longer use the original GLM architecture, they keep the name as a brand. The flagship release: **GLM 5.2**, an open-weight model that benchmarks between Opus 4.7 and 4.8 on the hardest tasks (SWE-bench Terminal Bench 2.1, long-horizon tasks). He showed direct comparisons on graphs that drew applause — specifically addressing rumors about whether GLM is close to frontier models.

A key innovation is a new "high" thinking budget level — responding to the observation that harder tasks consume more tokens. Even without thinking, the non-thinking 5.2 model outperforms the 5.1 thinking model — a significant leap for the open-weight category. GLM 5.2 leads the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index among open-weight models, close to frontier models, and excels beyond coding (GDP, math, role-play, general chat). He emphasized that GLM is more than a coding model — it's trained for general-purpose use and users should explore it for daily workflow beyond just coding tasks.

Zhuan Li articulated a principled open-weight philosophy: open for **security/control** (enterprise/government needs), **trust-building**, and **co-design** (customers and community co-shape the future). He explained that open-sourcing helps ZAI build trust with enterprises and governments who need to see the architecture and training recipe. Open-source success is attributed to ecosystem partners like Ollama, vLLM, and individual developers. He specifically thanked Peter Steinberger and other application builders — noting that open source includes not just models but software, tooling, and community support. He shared the GLM tech blog as a resource covering training pipeline, recipes, and architecture — there's much more behind the model than just great data.

The "one more thing" was **Z Code** — ZAI's own coding harness built for GLM 5.2 but supporting all frontier models with bring-your-own-key capabilities, compaction techniques, and workflow similar to Codex and Claude Code. He invited attendees to visit the ZAI booth for a live demo and expressed hope to attend in person next time.

### Key Knowledge Points

- **GLM 5.2**: Open-weight model between Opus 4.7-4.8; available now
- **Thinking Budgets**: New "high" thinking level; non-thinking 5.2 > thinking 5.1
- **Multi-Domain**: Coding + math + role-play + general chat — not just a coding model
- **Open Weight Philosophy**: Security, control, trust, co-design; ecosystem = true hero
- **Z Code**: ZAI's coding harness supporting all frontier models with bring-your-own-key
- **Tech Blog**: Covers training pipeline, recipe, and architecture details

### Follow-Up Items

- Download and test GLM 5.2 weights from Hugging Face
- Try the "high" thinking budget vs non-thinking comparison on your own evals
- Explore Z Code as an alternative coding harness
- Read GLM tech blog for training recipe insights
- Visit ZAI's booth to see Z Code demo
- Evaluate GLM 5.2 for non-coding workflows (role-play, chat, math)

## 6. Thomas Wolf (Hugging Face) & Olive (Minimax) — M3 & Native Multimodality

**Role:** Co-founder/CSO, Hugging Face & Researcher, Minimax  
**Time:** 10:21 AM

### Summary

Thomas Wolf (Hugging Face) interviewed Olive (Minimax) about their latest model, **M3** — a smaller model (~billions total, ~billions active parameters) that is unusually capable across coding, vision, and video with a 1-million-token context window. Thomas noted that between GLM (ranked #2 on Artificial Analysis) and now M3 (ranked #4), the audience was seeing the top open-source models in a row. M3 introduced **MiniMax Sparse Attention (MSA)**, an architecture designed to scale context length efficiently: an indexed branch selects what matters at a high level, and a sparse attention branch calculates on selected blocks — creating an elegant, scalable design. This architecture was actually designed by an intern, reflecting Minimax's unique bottom-up culture.

Olive shared that M3 is **natively multimodal** — trained from the first step on both text and vision data, not as an afterthought. This "native multi-modality" avoids the text-performance degradation and convergence issues that plague post-hoc multimodal training. She explained that most labs face model collapse when trying this approach, but Minimax solved it through work on ViT architecture, interleaved natural data (keeping images/videos in context instead of masking them out), careful data cleaning, and strong reward modeling that scales to large sizes without collapse. Thomas noted this is unusual — most labs don't even give interns access to training data, yet Minimax had an intern design a core architecture.

Internally, Minimax operates with a unique research culture: anyone can propose a project; if others are interested, they join for weeks or months; successful projects ship in the final model. Projects can be deep — architecture might require months of investigation before results materialize. Minimax also runs consumer apps reaching 100M+ individual users and 1M+ companies globally. Their CEO envisioned multimodal AGI from day one, before ChatGPT even existed. Apps exist as a means for people to experience model capabilities without needing to use APIs. Open-sourcing M3 is driven by belief that community feedback and PRs drive better future models. Olive confirmed they're already building M3.1 with community feedback incorporated, and future models will be much larger — potentially past trillion parameters.

### Key Knowledge Points

- **M3**: Small open-weight model (billions params), but top-5 open source; excellent coding + vision + video
- **1M Token Context**: Via MiniMax Sparse Attention (MSA) — scalable, elegant architecture
- **Native Multimodality**: Trained from step 1 on text + vision; avoids text-performance degradation
- **Intern-Designed Architecture**: MSA designed by an intern — unique bottom-up research culture
- **Scale**: 100M+ users, 1M+ companies across Minimax apps globally

## 8. Tisha & Sushin — Agent Determinism & Reproducibility

**Role:** Practitioners running agents against real production backends  
**Time:** 10:47 AM

### Summary

Tisha and Sushin tackled one of the most painful problems in production agent systems: **reproducibility**. They spoke from direct experience — both run agents against real production backends where "a battery isn't 'oh well, run it again'" — it's being on a call with a customer explaining where data went. They presented a vivid horror story: an agent told to "sell $1,000 of stock" instead passes the raw number "1000" to the quantity field, selling $190,000 worth of shares at $190/share. The API returns OK in milliseconds — zero exceptions, zero alerts. The dashboard is perfectly green. The trade is catastrophically wrong.

The gut reaction is to set temperature to zero. Their key insight: **this doesn't fix anything**. Temperature zero doesn't make a broken reasoning path correct — it makes the same logical error deterministically. Worse, temperature zero isn't even truly deterministic. They traced the problem to four first-principles sources: (1) sampling non-determinism — temperature zero means argmax, but underlying scores still vary run-to-run; (2) floating-point non-associativity — the order of decimal addition changes final logits, flipping the winning token; (3) batch variance — a request gets grouped with whatever else hits the server that millisecond; (4) mixture-of-experts routing — experts have strict capacity limits, and token routing depends entirely on the traffic in the batch. The ultimate takeaway: chasing identical token outputs is a losing battle.

They reframed the question entirely: the wrong question is "how do I make the model deterministic?" (teams burn weeks on this and walk away concluding the system is unknowable). The right question is "**how do I debug a run I can't reproduce?**" The key distinction is between **bitwise determinism** (same input → same output — unrealistic, undesirable, and you don't actually want it because randomness is what makes models creative) and **replayability** (rebuilding a run well enough to debug — achievable through observability). They advocate for recording all state transitions rather than chasing identical token outputs. The system needs to execute the same state transition, not return the exact same token.

### Key Knowledge Points

- **Temperature Zero Fallacy**: Doesn't fix reasoning errors; amplifies them deterministically
- **Four Sources of Non-Determinism**: Sampling, float math, batch variance, MoE routing
- **Wrong Question**: "How do I make the model deterministic?"
- **Right Question**: "How do I debug a run I can't reproduce?"
- **Bitwise Determinism vs Replayability**: Determinism is control (unrealistic); replayability is observability (achievable)
- **State Transitions**: Focus on system-level reproducibility, not token-level

### Follow-Up Items

- Audit your production agents for the "sell quantity" class of errors (unit mismatches)
- Remove temperature reduction as a debugging strategy — it's a red herring
- Implement replayability infrastructure: record full state transitions, not just outputs
- Build dashboards that detect semantic errors, not just API exceptions
- Read the referenced Hacker News/Reddit threads on GPU non-determinism

The key innovation: **adaptive flow control** — open at most one PR per loop at a time. If a human hasn't reviewed the last output, shut down (no reason to stack up un-reviewed work). - **Control Loops**: Borrowed from control theory — incremental change, constant measurement, feedback-based
- **Control Loop Components**: Set point → sensor → controller → actuator → system → disturbance dampener
- **AST-grep as Sensor**: Language-agnostic pattern matching for codebase state
- **Disturbance Dampener**: Prevent new violations from entering during migration

## Key Themes Across Speakers

1. **Loops Everywhere**: The conference's unifying theme — loopcraft, control loops, learning loops, inner/outer loops, software factory loops. The skill of moving between abstraction levels is the core AI engineer competency.

1. **The Human Role Evolves**: From writing code to designing loops. Humans decide **what** to build; agents handle **how**. The bottleneck shifts from tokens/compute to human tension/attention.

1. **From Factories to Orchestras**: A tension between factory automation (efficiency, scale) and human-centered orchestration (craft, flow, collaboration). Both models coexist.

1. **Model Agnosticism is Mandatory**: Locking into a single provider is a strategic mistake. Cost-per-capability-per-second, not token pricing, is the right metric.

1. **Retrieval & Memory Are the New Frontier**: With models commoditizing, the differentiator is how well your system retrieves, organizes, and learns from knowledge.

1. **Security is Foundational**: Not an afterthought — agent supply chain, identity, governance, and the "lethal trifecta" must be addressed for production scale.

1. **Cost is Structural**: Token costs and frontier pricing are barriers to factory adoption. Smart routing, caching, open-weight models, and CPUs-for-simple-tasks are the mitigation toolkit.

1. **Evaluation Must Evolve**: From output-correctness to system-behavior. Scenario-driven, production-telemetry-informed, continuous.

1. **Self-Improvement is the Goal**: The highest-leverage investment is loops that improve themselves — observer agents, skill loops, auto-memory-updating systems.

1. **The Bottleneck Keeps Moving**: Tokens → compute → tension → the next constraint. Engineers must keep recognizing where the bottleneck is and adapting their loops accordingly.
