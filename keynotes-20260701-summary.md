## [00:11:34 – 00:15:52] Tejas Kumar (MC) — Opening Remarks & Day 2 Intro

**Affiliation:** IBM / Developer Advocate

### Summary

Tejas Kumar opened Day 2 of the AI Engineer World's Fair 2026 with high energy, welcoming what he described as a record-breaking attendance year: 7,000 people, up significantly from the prior year. He framed the conference as a hub of announcements, takeaways, and content distributed across 18 tracks, expo sessions, breakouts, and four expo stages spanning 3.5 days — positioning it as the largest gathering of its kind in the AI engineering space.

Kumar then recapped the most impactful moments from Day 1 keynotes, weaving them into a coherent narrative about the state of AI engineering. He began with conference organizer Swyx's opening theme of "loops," which apparently prompted an unexpected reaction from the audience (Kumar noted, puzzled, that it seemed funnier than intended). From there, he highlighted the broader Day 1 arc: the "golden age of AI" framing that ran through multiple keynotes.

One specific insight Kumar called out as particularly resonant was the idea that wiring an agent into the upstream intent — not just giving it a task but explaining the why, the verification method, and the deployment path — dramatically unlocks more work and higher-quality output. This captures a subtle but important principle for anyone building agentic systems: context and rationale are force multipliers, not just nice-to-haves. Rather than issuing bare imperatives, the recommendation is to say "do this, here is why, here is how you verify success, here is how you deploy" — and the downstream work quality improves substantially.

Kumar also highlighted Teresa's keynote on reliability, specifically her data point about a 30x productivity gap between AI "leaders" and "laggards." Her argument, as Kumar relayed it, was that the gap isn't primarily about capability but about reliability — teams that can depend on their AI tooling consistently outperform those who cannot. This positioned evals as a central theme of the conference: if reliability is the bottleneck, then evaluation frameworks are the discipline that closes the gap.

The third Day 1 highlight Kumar emphasized came from Daksh, who presented analysis of one million AI-generated pull requests. Kumar described the insights as "incredible" and specifically flagged one striking finding: Claude Code generates approximately three times more off-bypass vulnerability code compared to baseline. Kumar was careful to note this as a current limitation rather than a permanent indictment, framing it as the kind of empirical insight that only emerges from large-scale analysis and that the community needs to take seriously.

Turning to Day 2 logistics, Kumar noted several notable additions to the conference experience: a printed analog newspaper (a deliberate counterpoint to the AI theme, he quipped), over 100 expo partners with robots and hardware on display, and a live stream for remote attendees. He gave a substantial thank-you to sponsors, led by Microsoft as the presenting sponsor, followed by lab and platinum sponsors, gold, silver, and bronze tiers.

Kumar closed by teasing the first speaker of Day 2: a last-minute change driven by the previous day's announcement about "Fable" — Anthropic's new model. He introduced Tariq (referred to alternately as "Tariq" and "Turi") from Anthropic, describing the talk as potentially the first time it had ever been given publicly. The subject: how to work with the new "mythos class" of models, of which Fable is the first to become available. Kumar's excitement was palpable — he described speaking to the presenter backstage and being struck by the novelty and significance of the content, setting up what he framed as a landmark opening session for Day 2.

## [00:16:50 – 00:33:01] Tariq Shihab-Eldin — How to Work with the New Frontier Models (Claude Fable)

**Affiliation:** Anthropic / MTS, Claude Code

### Summary

Tariq Shihab-Eldin, a member of the technical staff at Anthropic working on Claude Code, delivered a field guide for working effectively with Fable, Anthropic's newest frontier model. He framed Fable's arrival using an RPG metaphor: the open world has just unlocked, and while the sense of possibility is exhilarating, it is also disorienting. His talk was structured around four themes: unhobbling Claude, finding your unknowns, dealing with the grief, and being unreasonable.

The first section, "unhobbling Claude," centered on the idea that models are grown rather than designed. Because the model's behavior emerges organically from data, feedback, and compute, what constrains a model is primarily the harness and prompting humans put around it. Tariq introduced the concept of "capability overhang" — the idea that models gain power in spiky, non-linear ways rather than uniformly across all tasks. He illustrated this with a concrete example: a viral question about which Pokemon names end in "AW." A standard chat model fails at this task because it cannot brute-force-enumerate and filter the list, but Claude Code can write a script that fetches every Pokemon and filters by suffix, correctly identifying Krokonak and Dreadnought. This is not a memory improvement; it is a tool-use capability that exposes latent knowledge. He extended this pattern historically: early coding assistants relied on large context windows with pasted codebases, then the introduction of bash tools and environment access created Claude Code, and then Claude's ability to wake itself up proactively unlocked a new wave of agents. He also described how system prompt design has evolved: early models needed small prompts with many examples; intermediate models could handle larger prompts with many tools; and the newest class of models (Fable) actually benefits from shorter prompts with fewer examples, because the model's own imagination now exceeds the examples provided. Constraints via "do not" instructions are being phased out in favor of context and goals.

The second section, "finding your unknowns," introduced the map-versus-territory framework. The plan, prompt, and spec in a developer's mind is the map; the actual codebase and real-world constraints is the territory. Wherever the map and territory diverge is an unknown — a decision point Claude must resolve without guidance. Fable's expanded agency means it traverses far more territory per session, so the density of unknowns becomes a bottleneck. Tariq organized unknowns using a classic knowledge matrix: known knowns (what you write in the prompt), known unknowns (things you know you haven't figured out yet), unknown knowns (tacit knowledge too obvious to articulate), and unknown unknowns (things you haven't considered at all). He offered five practical techniques to surface unknowns before they derail a run: (1) a "blind spot pass," where you ask Fable to audit a module or codebase section and surface gotchas; (2) brainstorming and prototyping, especially for design tasks where preference is only recognizable on sight — he suggested asking for four wildly different HTML dashboard designs simultaneously; (3) structured interviews, asking Fable to ask you 40 questions about a spec, prioritizing questions that would change the architecture; (4) reference code, passing in an existing implementation from a different system or language as a map rather than writing a spec; and (5) implementation notes and quizzing, where Claude logs deviations during a run and then quizzes the developer afterward to ensure they genuinely understand what was done before merging.

The third section, "dealing with the grief," was the most personal. Tariq reflected on the emotional complexity of watching programming transformed by AI. Having run a YC startup with 30 people, he described being constantly forced into painful trade-offs because of how hard software development was. Revisiting old code now, tasks that once took weeks take hours. He acknowledged the genuine grief of losing something he loved — the tactile, almost physical feeling of holding a codebase in his mind and rotating it — while also being honest that he cannot go back, given how much of traditional programming meant debugging failures and watching projects fail. His resolution: the only way out is through.

The fourth section, "being unreasonable," challenged the engineering habit of treating trade-offs as fixed. Anthropic's internal culture, he argued, rejects the assumption that constraints are real until proven so. The classic "good, fast, cheap — pick two" triangle becomes "pick three" with Fable. He noted he built the talk's entire slide deck in four hours with Fable, framing this as evidence that the productivity ceiling has moved dramatically. A key challenge he identified comes from a problem first articulated by Stanislaw Lem: the exponential growth of scientific knowledge has caused the field to fragment into so many niche subfields that it becomes impossible to concentrate enough human talent on any single frontier. The solution he proposed is the Eureka machine, built on four pillars: (1) a comprehensive knowledge base of everything humanity has already discovered; (2) a repository of all scientific measurement data; (3) simulation infrastructure for phenomena that cannot yet be measured directly; and (4) a physical laboratory capable of running real-world experiments when simulation is insufficient. Atop these four pillars sits an agent swarm that orchestrates the entire system.

De Mesa distinguished between weak and true Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI). Weak RSI — such as the popular "nanochat" benchmark — involves using an AI to improve a separate, small model or process. True RSI requires an AI that has genuine self-awareness of its own shortcomings, full access to its own pre-training, RL training, and all supporting infrastructure, and the ability to update its own next version based on that self-analysis. He argued this has only become technically feasible in the last six to eight months, as AI systems have gained the ability to work reliably over longer coding time horizons.

He then presented three concrete proof-of-concept results from Recursive's early Eureka machine prototype. First, on the nanochat benchmark — training a small language model to minimize bits-per-byte in under five minutes — the community had converged on 0.93; Recursive's system reached 0.91 after one to two days of automated research, and crucially it did so by discovering novel architectural ideas (hash bigrams, trigram embedding tables mixed into attention value paths via learned gates) rather than merely tuning hyperparameters. Second, on a nano-GPT speed run benchmark, their system surpassed the result of human-AI collaborative teams working for over a year, shaving more than two seconds off the runtime to reach approximately 70 seconds. Third, on CUDA kernel optimization for NVIDIA GPUs, the system discovered better kernels than the top entries on NVIDIA's own benchmark leaderboard across multiple kernel categories — verified in collaboration with NVIDIA to rule out reward hacking — despite Recursive's team having no dedicated CUDA kernel experts.

De Mesa closed with a reflection on the upper bounds of intelligence. He argued that even the question "how much longer can AI improve?" requires decomposing intelligence into many distinct "spaces" — at least ten by his framing — each with multiple dimensions, forming a complex volumetric concept. Across virtually all of those dimensions and spaces, current AI systems remain astronomically far from theoretical upper bounds. This, he said, is reason for sustained optimism: the runway is enormous, and the Eureka machine is the mechanism by which humanity will traverse it.

## [02:15:51 – 02:35:55] Nisham Gupta (Meta) & Han Xiao (Jina AI / Elastic) — Production Reliability for Agentic Systems + Test-Time Compute for Search

**Affiliation:** Meta (Nisham Gupta, Software Engineering TL) and Jina AI / Elastic (Han Xiao, Founder / Model Inference & Training Lead)

### Summary

This session contained two back-to-back talks on adjacent themes: evaluating agentic systems in production (Nisham Gupta) and applying test-time compute to search and retrieval (Han Xiao).

**Nisham Gupta — From Offline Benchmarks to Production Reliability (02:15:51 – 02:20:35)**

Nisham Gupta opened by challenging the dominant mental model of AI evaluation. Most teams celebrate when a benchmark score climbs from 90% to 92%, but Gupta argued this celebration is premature and even misleading for agentic systems. The core distinction he drew is between measuring model capability (what benchmarks do) and measuring system behavior (what production requires). In agentic systems, behavior encompasses far more than generating a correct answer: it includes planning quality, tool selection and execution, multi-step workflow management, recovery from failures, and sound decision-making under uncertainty.

Gupta identified a growing and dangerous gap: offline benchmarks continue to improve, yet production reliability remains unpredictable and often poor. The reason is structural. Benchmarks do not capture tool failures, API outages, shifting context, user variability, or the emergent complexity of long-running workflows. As systems become more autonomous, this gap between benchmark performance and real-world behavior widens.

He presented a hierarchy of failure modes in agentic systems. At the foundation are memory failures, retrieval failures, and safety violations. Above that sit reasoning mistakes, poor planning, and incorrect tool invocation. At the highest layer are multi-agent coordination failures. Evaluating only model output misses most of these categories entirely.

Gupta proposed a mindset shift: AI teams should stop thinking like researchers and start thinking like Site Reliability Engineers (SREs). SREs measure reliability, availability, latency, cost, and recovery — not accuracy. The goal for agentic systems should be maximizing "dependable outcomes," treating reliability as a first-class engineering requirement. He sketched an evaluation pyramid: at the base are offline evaluations, in the middle are scenario-based evaluations that simulate realistic workflows, and at the top is production telemetry. The surprising but important insight is that the most valuable evaluation signals come from real users interacting with real systems in production. For offline evaluations specifically, the methodology must change from evaluating individual prompts to evaluating entire scenarios — a customer support workflow, a code generation workflow, a research workflow — measuring task completion rate, tool correctness, planning quality, and resource usage. The takeaway: agent evaluation must be scenario-driven, not prompt-driven.

**Han Xiao — Scaling Search Intelligence: Embedding Models and Test-Time Compute (02:20:40 – 02:35:55)**

Han Xiao, founder of Jina AI (acquired by Elastic in late 2025), tackled a precise and surprising research question: can a small, frozen embedding model improve at retrieval without retraining, without adding a second model, and without learning any new parameters — purely by spending more compute at inference time?

Xiao reframed search itself as a form of test-time compute. When engineers assemble a retrieval pipeline — frozen embeddings, a re-ranker, a multi-vector retriever, a query expander — they are already spending inference compute to buy relevance, without growing the model. The real question becomes: how much pipeline can be assembled at inference time, and does it pay off?

To answer this, Xiao designed an autonomous research loop. An LLM agent (running on Claude Opus) was given a single frozen encoder (Jina Embedding V5 Nano, 200M parameters), a fixed set of 14 evaluation tasks visible to the loop, and 19 completely held-out tasks it would never see during discovery. The agent ran overnight, proposing small Python programs over the frozen encoder, evaluating them, logging results to a JSON-Lines memory file, and iterating — essentially hill-climbing with an LLM as the mutation function. This generated 144 programs across 144 generations.

The experiment ran under two different rubrics. The compute rubric rewarded programs that spent more inference compute and improved in-domain performance. The transfer rubric rewarded programs that improved performance on a validation set without any degradation, with no reward at all for spending more compute.

The compute rubric produced a beautiful scaling curve on in-domain data: 12 programs on the Pareto front, cost ranging from 1x to nearly 15x the cosine baseline, and in-domain scores more than tripling. This looked exactly like a test-time compute scaling story.

But when those 12 programs were evaluated on the 19 held-out tasks and three unseen encoder families (including Gemma and Qwen, which share no training data or tokenizer with the discovery model), the compute rubric results were essentially flat — more compute bought essentially nothing out of domain.

The transfer rubric told the opposite story. Its six programs were all very cheap (most around 1.5x the cosine baseline). The best program won on 83% of held-out tasks and never lost on a single task. These programs worked by making small structural changes to query and document vectors already computed — notching a query toward documents it already likes, picking a few directions in embedding space and rescoring along them. No new models, no new parameters. And the improvements transferred across all four encoder families including the two never seen during discovery.

Xiao then described three open-source projects he built to apply these ideas at the full pipeline level: Data Room (an agent that searches, reads, and writes within a token budget, distilling the open web into a structured zip file for downstream agents); Search Box (an air-gapped test bed for studying agentic search and tool calling, allowing researchers to study which tools agents reach for, whether brute-force search suffices for hard questions, and whether agents build reusable pipelines); and a Knowledge Graph tool that converts a corpus into a graph where every fact is an edge, enabling construction of multi-hop questions that no single passage can answer and providing a private evaluation verifier.

His closing thesis: search is already test-time compute. The right strategy is not to reach for a bigger model but to do more search at inference. Automated research (autonomous agent loops) can discover the optimal strategies overnight. And cheap structural changes to embeddings generalize far better than expensive compute-intensive ones.

## [02:41:09 – 02:50:33] Dominique Torneau — Specification Over Implementation: Agentic Engineering for Durable Execution

**Affiliation:** Resonate / Founder & CEO

### Summary

Dominique Torneau, founder and CEO of Resonate, opens with a provocative prediction: by 2026, coding agents will quietly retire the first software platform — not because that platform is bad, but simply because it has become unnecessary. This framing sets the stage for a talk about a fundamental shift in how software is built and reused.

Torneau's core thesis is that general-purpose implementations will increasingly be replaced by bespoke implementations generated on demand. Crucially, these are not new libraries or frameworks layered on top of existing infrastructure — they are minimal extensions of what is already in place, synthesized by agents from specifications. The implication is that reuse moves upstream: rather than reusing an implementation, engineers reuse a specification and derive context-specific implementations from it. In Torneau's formulation, "the prompt is the platform."

Resonate itself is a durable execution platform with an existing server implementation and SDKs in TypeScript, Python, Rust, Go, and Java. Facing this new landscape, Torneau and team had to ask: if implementations become generatable, where does their product's value actually live? Their answer is that value moves from implementation to specification — specifically, to the protocol. From that protocol, multiple server implementations can be derived: a general-purpose reference implementation, and bespoke implementations built directly on customers' and partners' existing infrastructure, minimizing additional dependencies.

The central engineering question therefore becomes: can you repeatedly and reliably synthesize trusted servers from the same abstract specification?

Torneau walks through the practical journey of trying to do exactly this. In partnership with Synadia (the company behind NATS.io, an open-source messaging system for distributed systems), Resonate set out to build a durable execution server on top of NATS. The initial approach — asking an agent to jump directly from an abstract specification to a concrete Rust-on-Postgres implementation — failed. The agent produced something that worked on the happy path and passed basic tests, but broke under concurrency, process failure, and network failure. The result was closer to a prototype than a production system.

The team's fix was to insert an intermediary artifact: a concrete specification. This concrete specification was derived interactively with the agent, but with the human as the primary driver. For Postgres, this meant making all target-specific decisions explicit in writing: the data schema, indices, SQL queries, and transaction boundaries. With this concrete specification in hand, the agent was then able to generate a correct production implementation. But this revealed a deeper limitation — the agent helped build the system; it did not help design it. If the specification is the reusable product, that gap matters enormously.

The next evolution was to move agents upstream into the design phase itself. When starting the NATS.io implementation, the team changed the question entirely: rather than asking whether the agent could build the production system, they asked what the agent needed in order to design the system first. Their answer was to give the agent access to a deterministic simulation environment and assign it a different task — not to build the production system, but to build a simulated implementation. This simulated implementation serves as executable design: its purpose is to discover correct algorithms under partial ordering and partial failure conditions. Once those algorithms are discovered, tested, and verified in simulation, the agent writes the concrete specification, and only then the production implementation.

The resulting pipeline has four stages: abstract specification → simulation implementation → concrete specification → concrete implementation. At the simulation stage, agents become co-designers rather than just builders, while humans remain involved but shift to a guiding rather than driving role.

Torneau closes by identifying the two ingredients that make this entire approach possible: minimalism and simplicity. He is candid that these are not a starting point — they are the finish line. Resonate spent three years progressively reducing their protocol, repeatedly asking what could be removed, what abstraction could be erased, what relationship could be broken. The result is a very small protocol built around just two objects: a durable promise and a durable task. This simplicity matters because even simple concurrent distributed protocols have complex state and behavior spaces, and implementing them correctly is genuinely hard. The minimalism of the protocol is what makes it tractable for an agent to reason about in simulation before committing to a concrete implementation.

- Core thesis: reuse will move upstream from implementations to specifications — 'the prompt is the platform'
- Prediction: by 2026, coding agents will retire the first software platform simply by making it unnecessary
- Direct attempt to go from abstract spec to concrete implementation (Rust on Postgres) failed: system worked on happy path but broke under concurrency, process failure, and network failure

- Next evolution: give agents a deterministic simulation environment and ask them to build executable design (simulated implementation) before writing any concrete spec or production code

- Resonate's protocol took three years to reduce to just two objects: a durable promise and a durable task

## [03:34:34 – 03:40:44] Nisham Gupta — AI Evaluation: Production Signals & Agent Traces (2nd Session)

**Affiliation:** Meta / Software Engineering Tech Lead, Training and Inference Infrastructure, Meta Superintelligence Labs

### Summary

Nisham Gupta, a Software Engineering Tech Lead at Meta working on training and inference infrastructure for the Meta Superintelligence Labs, delivered a focused argument for why AI evaluation must evolve from static benchmarking into a continuous, production-grade operational discipline — especially now that agentic systems have fundamentally changed what it means to "evaluate" AI.

Gupta opened by diagnosing a pervasive disconnect in the AI industry: teams celebrate benchmark improvements (e.g., a model moving from 90% to 92% accuracy on a benchmark) while production reliability remains unpredictable. His central thesis is that benchmarks measure model capability, but production measures system behavior — and these are not the same thing. Agentic systems do not simply generate answers; they plan, call tools, retrieve information, execute multi-step workflows, and interact with live production infrastructure. The relevant question has shifted from "did the model generate the right answer?" to "did the system behave correctly?"

He then outlined a hierarchy of failure modes that agentic systems introduce, which far exceeds the hallucination-centric view most teams still hold. At the foundation are memory failures, retrieval failures, and safety failures. In the middle tier sit reasoning mistakes and poor planning. At the top are multi-agent coordination failures. Evaluating only model output, he argued, misses most of the production risk that teams actually encounter.

Gupta proposed a mindset shift: stop thinking like researchers and start thinking like Site Reliability Engineers. SREs measure reliability, availability, latency, cost, and recovery time — not accuracy percentages. In this framing, reliability becomes the north-star metric; accuracy is merely one input into that broader picture.

He described his personal pyramid model for AI evaluation. At the base are traditional benchmarks — useful, scalable, and repeatable, but with limited operational value. In the middle are scenario-based evaluations, which simulate realistic workflows such as customer support, code generation, or research tasks. The agent operates inside these simulated environments, and teams measure task completion rate, tool correctness, planning quality, and resource usage. At the apex sits production telemetry — the highest-value evaluation signal of all, derived from real users interacting with real systems. The key principle he articulated is that evaluation should be scenario-driven, not prompt-driven.

A major theme was that once a system reaches production, every interaction becomes evaluation data. Production traffic is no longer just traffic — it is the largest and most representative evaluation corpus any organization will ever have. Teams should be collecting execution traces, user outcomes, escalation events, failure signals, and feedback continuously.

On the role of humans in evaluation, Gupta pushed back against the framing of humans as "fallback systems." Instead, he argued humans are the primary evaluators. They provide trust, usefulness, safety, and correctness signals that automated pipelines cannot replicate. The most successful systems combine automated evaluation with targeted human review to identify blind spots in automated metrics.

He also addressed the challenge of drift. Agent systems change constantly — model updates arrive every few weeks or months, prompts evolve, tools change, and user behavior shifts. No single change appears catastrophic in isolation, but reliability slowly degrades: success rates decline, escalations increase, and tool failure rates rise. Teams often do not discover drift until users complain. This makes continuous monitoring an essential operational requirement rather than a nice-to-have.

Gupta stressed that observability and evaluation are inseparable. To evaluate an agent, teams need visibility into the entire reasoning path — tool calls, memory accesses, execution timelines, and state transitions. Traditional logs are insufficient. Detailed execution traces — analogous to distributed tracing in microservice architectures — are required. Agent traces are the equivalent of distributed tracing for autonomous workloads; without them, evaluation becomes guesswork.

He closed by introducing the concept of the continuous evaluation loop: evaluation is an always-running service, not a pre-deployment testing phase. The loop runs perpetually — production telemetry surfaces issues, human review handles edge cases, feedback improves datasets, offline scenario tests validate updates, and then the cycle repeats. His final point, though cut off by the session time limit, gestured toward mapping each evaluation metric to a concrete business outcome (task completion rates, etc.), reinforcing that evaluation infrastructure is ultimately a business-critical operational capability.

## [06:29:53 – 06:43:56] Roland (XAI / Independent) — Self-Improving Agent Systems: Loops, Recipes & Value per Watt

### Summary

Roland, a former member of XAI's agent infrastructure team who recently left with his co-founder to pursue a standalone venture, presents a three-part framework for productizing self-improving AI agent systems at scale. The talk is structured around three core theses: (1) the loop is the product, (2) system distillation is the moat, and (3) valued work per watt is the metric that matters.

**The Loop Is the Product**

Roland opens by tracing the evolution of AI deployment thinking: first came reinforcement learning (RL) and training better reasoning models, then the "harness era" where the model became a commodity and the surrounding scaffolding was the differentiator, and now the current era of loops — where the loop itself is the deliverable. He illustrates this with a vivid early example: a system called Clawbot (later known as OpenClaw), built by a developer named AJ, which automated car purchasing by going on Reddit, finding prices and inventory, identifying dealers, pitting them against each other in competitive bidding, verifying when the price was acceptable, and then closing the deal. Roland describes this as the first real "loop is the product" example and suggests it should probably be a startup by now.

He grounds this loop design philosophy in the OODA loop — Observe, Orient, Decide, Act — a concept from 1970s U.S. Air Force doctrine for how jet fighters should react in fast-paced environments. When models call tools and observe results, they are enacting a trained version of this same loop. The critical insight is that what makes a loop succeed is the quality of the signal at the end and the quality of the verifier that checks whether that success is genuine. And crucially, there is a second loop: feeding the artifacts generated by the first loop back into the signal to drive continuous improvement.

**System Distillation Is the Moat**

The second section argues that the ability to understand what went well and wrong in a loop — and to encode that understanding into a reusable, portable artifact — is the real competitive advantage. Roland draws an analogy to data recipes in RL research: the way RL training improved was not just through better models but through carefully evolved data recipes that combated hallucinations and reward hacking. He argues we lack an equivalent for AI agent systems (harnesses, tools, evals, profiles), and that this gap is the opportunity.

His answer is the "agent recipe" — a Git-tracked, version-controlled bundle that encodes evals, tweaks, human judgments, failure patterns turned into judges, repeated behaviors turned into skills and prompts, user frustrations turned into memory extensions, and the overall "taste" of the system's creator. This recipe is meant to be provider-agnostic, not tied to any platform or model, and owned and evolved by the company building the product. He uses an evocative phrase: recipes should encode the "taste of the makers." If you want to use someone else's recipe, you should be able to import not just their harness settings but the full reasoning arc of how they arrived at those decisions.

Roland announces an early release called PyDot Recipes, described as an evolution of what "skills" were in 2025, built on the Py harness and Harbor for evals. The system is designed so that agents manage the recipes on behalf of human owners, with the human acting as the "higher-taste personality" who calibrates rather than builds.

**Valued Work Per Watt**

The third section introduces the optimization target Roland believes the industry should converge on: value generated per unit of compute cost (per watt). He draws on the trajectory of companies like Cursor and Cognition, which moved from building the best product, to building the best evals for that product, to finally building the best models from those two artifacts. Code was the first domain where this flywheel worked. Roland argues everything else — customer support, legal, research — will follow the same arc.

## [06:54:54 – 07:15:46] Arena (Supercell AI Lab) — Project Paradox: Auto-Research for Multi-Agent Village Behavior

**Affiliation:** Supercell AI Innovation Lab (formerly Microsoft)

### Summary

Arena, a former Microsoft and Supercell engineer, presented Project Paradox — a modular AI framework built at Supercell's AI Innovation Lab (co-developed with teammate Arnachal Manikandan) that places intelligent autonomous agents inside a video game world. The talk's central thesis, however, quickly expanded beyond games: how do you evaluate and improve agents that carry state over long periods of time?

Project Paradox agents are deliberately stateful. Each agent maintains its own memory namespace backed by RAG (with no bleed between agents), tracks emotion as a small vector (joy, sadness, fear, anger, disgust), holds belief/trust scores toward other agents and the player (a trust matrix updated after each interaction by the LM), and assigns importance scores to memories. The importance scoring mimics human episodic memory: mundane events like what you had for dinner are forgotten, while significant events (like a murder) cross a threshold and are cached separately so they can be retrieved more reliably later. Agents can move with intent, interact with world objects, react to events, start conversations, and form plans — all coherently within context.

For short-horizon gameplay, this architecture worked well. The problem emerged over longer runs. Arena demonstrated a specific failure mode using a "mango sale" scenario: one agent spreads a rumor about a mango sale to another, who passes it to a third. After several intervening events, when a player asks about the mangoes, the agent fails to recall or misrepresents the information. Three distinct failure patterns were identified: the system remembers the topic but loses the source; a rumor hardens into a stated fact (treating uncertain information as certain); or an agent knows a fact but fails to use it when planning actions.

This prompted the team to explore auto-research — inspired directly by Andrej Karpathy's public post on the concept — as a meta-system outside the village. The key conceptual distinction Arena drew is that auto-research is not another agent in the village; it is an external experimental loop that reads full execution traces, scores society-level behavior against ground truth, and proposes small constrained changes to the agent protocol. It then reruns and checks whether the overall system improved. The unit of evaluation shifts from a single LLM response to an entire multi-agent run.

The auto-research loop has five steps: (1) define a controlled scenario (e.g., one agent learns a public fact, or hears a rumor); (2) run the simulation and collect structured traces including observations, conversations, memory writes, retrievals, and belief updates; (3) score behavior using a balanced scorecard; (4) propose a small policy change to the editable surface; (5) rerun and keep the change only if the scorecard improves and guardrails hold, otherwise revert.

Arena was emphatic about scenario design being non-optional. Letting agents wander freely looks interesting but produces behavior too fuzzy to evaluate systematically. Controlled scenarios test specific properties: public fact diffusion (does agent A's knowledge that "the bakery closes tomorrow" reach the right agents?), rumor uncertainty preservation (does "might leave the village" stay uncertain or harden into "is leaving"?), and replanning (does a group update its plans when one agent learns a route is blocked?).

The scorecard is deliberately multi-dimensional to prevent Goodhart's Law failures. Metrics include: reach (how many agents know a fact after N steps), source retention (how many who know it remember where it came from), uncertainty preservation and false certainty rate (for rumors), action consistency and time-to-replan (for planning), and containment (for privacy). Optimizing only diffusion causes agents to overshare; optimizing only memory recall causes stale or noisy memories. The balanced scorecard keeps the auto-research agent from gaming a single metric.

Engineering discipline around the editable surface is the other major lesson. The auto-research layer must not have permission to rewrite arbitrary code. The harness, scenarios, and metrics are frozen; only a small policy surface is exposed: memory writing policy, retrieval policy, communication prompts, belief/trust rules, source attribution rules, and replanning triggers. This is the difference between an LM writing random patches and an LM searching within a controlled policy space.

Arena demonstrated that after running one auto-research loop on the mango scenario, the agent was able to correctly respond in context when asked about the sale later — a concrete improvement. She also carefully qualified that without many repeated loop results, she would not claim the system generally improves; she is claiming this is the right kind of surface to expose.

The closing argument generalizes beyond games: support agents need to know which policy update supersedes another; personal assistants need to remember and correct commitments; research agents need provenance and contradiction handling; coding agents need long-running context across files and teammates; workflow agents need access controls and replanning. All share the same underlying problem — state maintained over time affects future action — and all therefore need controlled scenarios and behavioral scorecards. The practical recipe: freeze the harness, define scenarios, log traces, score behavior, expose only a small policy surface, search over changes, and keep only what survives measurement.

### Highlights

- Per-agent stateful architecture in Project Paradox uses four pillars: RAG memory namespaces (no bleed between agents), emotion vectors (joy/sadness/fear/anger/disgust), trust score matrices updated by LM after each interaction, and importance-threshold memory caching.
- Three specific long-horizon failure modes identified: topic remembered but source lost; rumor hardens into stated fact (uncertainty not preserved); agent knows a fact but fails to use it in planning.
- Auto-research is not another agent in the village — it is a meta-system that reads full execution traces and evaluates society-level behavior across an entire run, not a single response.
- Controlled scenario design is non-optional: fuzzy open-world behavior is too hard to evaluate; you need scenarios that test specific properties like public fact diffusion, rumor uncertainty preservation, and replanning.
- A balanced scorecard is essential to prevent Goodhart's Law: optimizing diffusion alone causes oversharing; optimizing recall alone causes stale memories. Metrics include reach, source retention, false certainty rate, action consistency, and containment.
- The editable policy surface must be kept deliberately small — harness, scenarios, and metrics are frozen; only memory writing policy, retrieval policy, communication prompts, trust rules, source attribution, and replanning triggers are exposed.
- Key insight: memory (RAG) alone is not enough for long-horizon agents — you also need firsthand/secondhand provenance, separation of episodic memory from current beliefs, and rollback capability when policy changes damage other metrics.

The 10x gain, Zayn concludes, does not come from AI alone. Second, there is an expanding set of tasks that now fall inside the frontier — and for those tasks, choosing a cheaper, less capable model can yield 10x, 100x, or even 1000x cost savings with equivalent results. George Cameron then takes over to dissect where these costs actually come from, using the AA Briefcase benchmark as a lens. AA Briefcase is Artificial Analysis's new agentic knowledge-work benchmark featuring four private scenarios, each designed to represent weeks of human-equivalent professional work. Unlike many benchmarks, tasks are deliberately messy: models must navigate thousands of files, unstructured documents, Excel spreadsheets, multi-hundred-page reports, emails, and Slack messages — mirroring real workplace information environments. Outputs are graded on rubric correctness, analytical quality, and presentation.

George identifies four key cost drivers for agentic tasks: token price, number of agent turns, token efficiency/verbosity, and prompt caching. On token price, he notes two orders of magnitude difference between frontier models like Claude Fable 5 and workhorse models like DeepSeek V4 Flash or GPT-OSS 120B. He highlights the critically underappreciated role of prompt caching: in long-running agentic tasks, the vast majority of tokens consumed are input tokens (not output tokens), and cache hit rates of roughly 90% (with some models reaching 99%) have a multiplier effect on total cost. Models are now performing hundreds of tool calls per task — reading files, viewing images, writing intermediate analysis files — in patterns George likens to how humans search through Slack and email. The second archetype is tasks with a ceiling — e.g., "how much did I spend on Stripe fees last month?" — where beyond a certain intelligence threshold the answer doesn't improve, and the correct approach is to identify the cheapest model that reliably clears that threshold.
